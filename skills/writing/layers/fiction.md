# Fiction

Run the self-check with `--fiction`.

## The brief is material, not a script

- Change the order, start late, and show, imply or skip facts that only explain. Leave out details that don't fit, and add small ones the brief didn't give: a habit, an object, a remark. Aim for a little sparkle, not wild invention.
- If the brief sets limits (no new events or characters), stay inside them.
- Give the story a title only it could have. Write times, ages and jobs as the character would think them, and only where they matter to this story.

## People with lives

- Every character has a life outside the story and wants something. Let a habit, a history or an old grudge leak into how they act and talk. One physical detail per character is enough.
- The reader feels with the characters, or deliberately doesn't when the story calls for distance.
- Give the reader something to learn: trade knowledge, how a thing really works, an odd fact a character would know. The best stories leave a small "wow" about the people, the world or the plot.
- Allow humour: a dry remark, a small absurdity, a short anecdote that fits the character. Not everything is a joke, and the humour stays true to the people.
- Let people talk. Use "said" or an action beat. People deflect, understate, answer the wrong question, and don't all sound articulate.

## Prose that moves

- Write in full sentences. No runs of fragments ("No spare. No time."), and no fragment at all unless it is a line of dialogue.
- Vary the sentences. Mix real long sentences with short ones. A long sentence carries an action through its steps with subordinate clauses (when, while, because, until) and ends on what matters. Several short sentences in a row read like a list.
- No semicolons in narration. Use a conjunction, a relative clause or a new sentence.
- Don't round off a paragraph with a short verdict ("This one she didn't know.", "It was over."). End on an action or a detail.
- At the peak, let the narration take on the character's words and judgments, without "she thought".
- Cut filter verbs ("she heard"). Use a sense only where it changes what someone does.

## Surprise and endings

- An attentive reader may guess roughly where it goes, but never easily and never how. Plant the payoff early and let it land where they didn't look.
- Don't resolve every tension or explain what the brief leaves open. End on something the reader completes, not a summary, a moral or a receding view.
- Avoid model-default images: a machine that "finds a voice", lights shrinking into the distance, kitchen similes for mechanical noise. Pick what only this character would notice.
- Stock phrases to avoid, the most over-represented in model fiction: "voice barely a whisper", "took a deep breath", "couldn't help but feel", "heart pounding in her chest", "air thick with the scent of", "eyes widened", "brow furrowed", "casting long shadows". Use one concrete physical detail that belongs to this character and scene.
